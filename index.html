01 — About

Engineering that answers to reality.

Most of my work lives where software meets consequences. At NAXA, I built the backend of Nepal's national weather forecast system for the Department of Hydrology & Meteorology — pipelines that pull ECMWF and NOAA model data, correct it, and turn it into SMS and voice alerts that reach households before rivers rise.

Before that, at KrispCall (YC-backed), I designed VoIP microservices keeping 99.9% uptime for 10,000+ concurrent users, wired up six CRM integrations, and learned that idempotency is a love language. I also lead software for Nepal's first open-source humanoid robot at HTP Lab — speech, vision, and a firm handshake.

Outside the terminal you'll find me running the trails around Kathmandu, judging hackathons in the Terai, or in a café corner with a physical book — usually Taleb or Durant, always arguing with the author.

04 — Lab Notes

Building a robot that notices things.

At HTP Lab in Kathmandu — guided by Muni Sakya, Nepal's first computer manufacturer — a small team and I are assembling an InMoov upper-body humanoid from 3D-printed parts, servos, and stubbornness.

My side of the project is the mind: a Python master control system that fuses dual-camera vision with a vision-language model, Whisper for speech, an LLM reasoning layer with fallback chains, and Kokoro TTS for voice. DeepFace handles face and emotion recognition, while magnetic encoders and accelerometers give the robot proprioception — knowing where its own joints are.

The unglamorous work matters most: calibrating encoder values by hand, computing tyre revolution ratios for wheel odometry, tuning until the handshake feels like one.

08 — Principles

Things I believe, provisionally.

१

The river doesn't read the documentation.

Build for the monsoon, not the demo. Reality is the only test suite that never gets skipped.

२

A warning that arrives late is just a report.

In early-warning work, latency is an ethical question. Every second in the pipeline belongs to someone downstream.

३

Stress is information.

What breaks a system tells you what it was pretending to be. Let the failures teach, then design so they can't repeat.

४

Read with a pencil.

Every book is an argument. I keep my side of it in the margins.

५

Teach what you just learned.

A thousand students later, I'm sure of this: explaining something is the fastest way to find where you don't understand it yet.

६

Walk in circles, think in straight lines.

The kora around Boudha settles more of my design decisions than any whiteboard does.
